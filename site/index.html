Building Muscle as a Teenager

Evidence-based guidance for young people who want to train seriously — without the myths, the marketing, or the shortcuts.

⏱ Set Your Expectations First In 2025, scientists looked at all the research on muscle growth. If you are new to training, you can expect to gain about 1 to 2 kg of muscle in 8 to 12 weeks. Growth then slows down as you get more experienced.

That is a good result. It is also much slower than fitness videos make it look. You measure this in months, not weeks. Anyone promising faster is selling you something, taking drugs, or showing you photos taken years apart.

Source: Van Every et al. (2025), Journal of Sport and Health Science.

What Really Sets the Pace

The honest answer to "how fast can I get big" is that it partly depends on something you cannot control.

The best answer comes from a group called the American Academy of Pediatrics. Paediatrics is the branch of medicine that deals with children and teenagers, so these are doctors who specialise in people your age. This site refers to them as the children's doctors from here on.

Their report describes two stages. Before puberty, you get stronger mainly because your brain gets better at switching on the muscle you already have. You get stronger without looking much bigger. During and after puberty, your hormones change and real muscle growth becomes possible on top of that.

The report is blunt about what this means. There is no substitute for puberty starting, and training harder will not make it happen sooner.

Two people the same age can be at completely different stages. So comparing yourself to someone in your year tells you very little.

💡 Why this is worth knowing If you are training properly and getting stronger, but you do not look different yet, nothing is going wrong. That is the normal order things happen in.

Source: Stricker, Faigenbaum & McCambridge (2020), Pediatrics — American Academy of Pediatrics clinical report, reaffirmed November 2024.

Who This Site Is For

This guide is for teenagers who are new to lifting weights, or in their first year or two.

The doctors' guidance behind it covers children and teenagers generally. It does not set a minimum age. What matters more is whether you can follow instructions and train with someone supervising you.

This is not a bodybuilding site and it is not selling anything. Where the evidence is weak, or where the research was done on adults instead of teenagers, this site says so on the page. Every claim points back to a source you can check on the Sources page.

This website is an educational resource made for a school Extended Project Qualification. It is not a substitute for advice from a doctor or a dietitian. The NHS says children and young people should get medical advice about exercising if they have a health condition. If you have any health condition, injury, or worry, speak to your GP before you start training.

🔬 How Muscles Grow

What really makes muscle grow, and which popular explanations the evidence no longer backs up.

What Is Hypertrophy?

Hypertrophy is the word for muscle growth. It means a muscle fibre getting thicker.

Imagine slicing straight through a muscle, like cutting through a piece of rope. Hypertrophy means that slice gets bigger. Scientists call the size of that slice the cross-sectional area. It happens mainly because your body packs more of the tiny strands that do the pulling inside each fibre.

One more thing worth knowing: you do not build muscle during the workout itself. Training is the signal. The building happens afterwards.

Source: Van Every et al. (2025), Journal of Sport and Health Science.

What Really Makes Muscle Grow

Older training articles say three things drive growth: tension, the burn, and muscle damage. In 2025, scientists reviewed the evidence to separate what really works from ideas that just sound right. Their answer is narrower.

THIS ONE WORKS

Mechanical Tension

The pulling force on the muscle when you lift something heavy. This is the main thing that makes muscle grow. Tiny structures inside the fibre can sense that pull, a bit like a stretch sensor. They then send a chemical message that tells the body to start building.

THIS ONE DOESN'T

The Burn and the Pump

The burning feeling during high-rep sets, and the swollen feeling afterwards. The review found no good evidence that these make muscle grow. They happen while you work hard, but they are not the reason you grow. Chasing the pump is not a training plan.

NOBODY IS SURE

Muscle Damage

The idea that training tears the muscle and it heals back thicker. The review gives the credit to tension instead, and does not treat damage as a cause. But it never set out to test the damage idea properly. The paper that does is not free to read. So this site says the question is still open rather than pretending to know.

💡 What this means for your training If tension is what counts, a set can work even when it felt fairly easy. What matters is that you moved a challenging weight through a full range. You do not need a burn, a pump, or to be sore the next day.

Source: Van Every et al. (2025), Journal of Sport and Health Science. The older three-part explanation comes from Schoenfeld (2010) and is still repeated widely online.

The Hormone Myth

A lot of training advice is built on one idea: that certain workouts cause a spike in hormones like testosterone, and that the spike is what builds muscle.

The 2025 review found this is not true. The short rise in testosterone and growth hormone after a workout does not affect muscle growth, in men or in women.

Here is the evidence that is hardest to argue with. Women have far less testosterone than men. Yet from the same training, they gain a similar percentage increase in muscle and strength.

Source: Van Every et al. (2025), Journal of Sport and Health Science.

How Often to Train, Not How Long to Rest

Fitness sites often give a fixed rest time. Wait 48 hours. Allow 72 hours to repair. The guidance this site follows does not work that way.

It gives you a number of sessions per week instead. That is easier to follow, and it is what the studies actually measured.

2–3×
Sessions a week, with rest days in between (guidance for children and teenagers)
≥2×
Train each major muscle group per week (guidance for adults)

The adult guidance adds something useful. Once you train a muscle twice a week, adding more sessions makes little difference, as long as the total amount of work stays the same. So there is nothing to gain from squeezing in extra sessions.

Sources: Stricker, Faigenbaum & McCambridge (2020), Pediatrics; Currier et al. (2026), ACSM Position Stand, Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise — the second was done on adults aged 18 and over.

📈 Training Principles

The rules that separate useful training from just being tired.

📋 Read this before the numbers below The numbers on this page come from the biggest review of training ever put together. It pulled in 137 other reviews covering more than 30,000 people. But every one of those people was an adult aged 18 or over. Teenagers were left out on purpose.

So these numbers are the best guess we have for a teenager, not a proven answer. Where guidance written for young people exists, it is on the Safety page, and that guidance wins.

1. Progressive Overload — Real, But Smaller Than You Are Told

Your muscles adapt to what you ask of them. Progressive overload means slowly asking for more over time.

It does not only mean adding weight. The adult guidance counts all of these as progress: more weight, more sets, more sessions, different exercises, or longer workouts. Adding one rep counts. So does doing the same set with better form.

Here is the part usually left out. The same review found you do not need to progress to benefit from training. Progression is mainly needed by people chasing long-term gains. In your first year, just turning up regularly does most of the work.

📓 Write It Down Note what you lifted, how many reps, and how it felt. You cannot tell if you are progressing if you cannot remember last week. Seeing your progress written down also turns out to be the part of training with the best evidence behind it for confidence.

Source: Currier et al. (2026), ACSM Position Stand, Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise (adults 18+).

2. For Size, How Much Beats How Heavy

The adult guidance splits two goals that people usually mix up.

Getting bigger does not depend much on how heavy you lift. It depends mostly on how much work you do in total. Getting stronger is the goal that needs heavy weights.

That is good news if you are starting out. You do not need to find out your maximum lift in order to grow. That is why this site never tells you to lift a percentage of your maximum.

≥10
Sets per muscle group per week for growth
18–20
Point where extra sets stop helping much
2–3
Training days a week for teenagers

The review also found you do not need to push a set until you physically cannot do another rep. Stopping with two or three reps left in the tank works just as well.

Sources: Currier et al. (2026), ACSM Position Stand (adults 18+) for the set numbers; Stricker et al. (2020), Pediatrics and Zwolski et al. (2017), Sports Health for the 2–3 days figure in young people.

3. "Time Under Tension" — A Myth You Will See Everywhere

You will see people online say that slowing every rep down grows more muscle, because the muscle spends longer under tension.

The review tested it and found nothing. Reps lasting about half a second grew just as much muscle as reps lasting eight seconds. That is a sixteen-fold difference in time, and no difference in the result.

There is a catch too. Slowing every rep down forces you to use a lighter weight. So you spend longer doing less.

Source: Currier et al. (2026), ACSM Position Stand (adults 18+).

4. Deload Weeks — A Habit, Not a Finding

Taking an easier week every four to six weeks is normal practice in gyms, and the programme on this site includes one.

It is worth being straight about where that comes from. None of the sources this site uses shows that deload weeks work. The adult review lists the things that reliably improve results, and deloads are not on the list.

This is a habit, not evidence. It is here because taking a week easier is low-risk and lots of people do it, not because a study showed it grows more muscle. If you skip it, you are not doing anything wrong.

5. Doing Too Much

More training is not automatically more progress. Past a point it goes the other way.

Research on young athletes gives a rule that is easy to remember. Count your hours of organised sport and training each week. If that number is higher than your age in years, you are more likely to pick up an overuse injury. So if you are 17, more than 17 hours a week is the danger zone.

Two or three gym sessions a week is nowhere near that. The risk here belongs to people training every day across several sports.

Two things worth knowing

  • Growing quickly and training hard both raise your injury risk. When they happen at the same time, the risk adds up.
  • Your coordination actually gets slightly worse in the six months or so before your fastest growth spurt. The time you are growing quickest is the time your movement is least under control. Holding the weight steady and working on technique through that stretch is the sensible move.
⚠️ If training keeps leaving you exhausted, or you keep getting niggling injuries Talk to a parent, a coach, or your GP rather than pushing through. This site cannot tell you what is wrong. The honest advice is to ask someone who can.

Source: Sugimoto et al. (2024), HSS Journal — this studied young people in organised sport generally, not weight training specifically.

🏋️ The Best Exercises

Learn the basic movements first. The exercise list below is my choice, and this page says which parts are which.

How These Exercises Were Chosen

There is almost never a study behind one specific exercise. What the research does support is an order to do things in.

Start with the basic movement patterns, like the squat and the hip hinge. Get the technique right with little or no weight. Add weight after that. Programmes that worked well with young people usually used six to eight exercises in a session.

What is sourced, and what is my opinion. The order above comes from the research. The exercises in the tables below are my choice. I picked movements that fit those patterns, that you can learn without a coach standing over you, and that work in a normal gym or at home. Treat the list as a sensible starting point, not a scientific ranking.

Sources: McQuilliam et al. (2020), Sports Medicine; Zwolski, Quatman-Yates & Paterno (2017), Sports Health; Stricker et al. (2020), Pediatrics.

Compound vs Isolation

Compound exercises use several joints and several muscle groups at once. A squat is a good example. They let you move more weight with more muscle, which is the stimulus that matters. They also cover the basic patterns you should learn first.

Isolation exercises work one muscle at a time. A bicep curl is an example. They are useful for filling in gaps.

How much of each should you do? None of the sources on this site answers that for teenagers. So any split you see quoted online is somebody's preference, not a finding. That includes the 80/20 rule, which comes up a lot.

Working Towards Your First Chin-Up

The chin-up is the move most people want and cannot do yet. So here is a method rather than a target.

Jump or step up to the top position, then lower yourself as slowly as you can control. Do this on the days your programme includes pulling, along with band-assisted reps.

No timescale here, on purpose. Fitness sites often say most people get their first chin-up in four to eight weeks. I could not find any research behind that. How long it takes depends on your bodyweight, your starting strength, and where you are in puberty. The honest answer is that it varies a lot.

The same goes for the "how many should I be able to do at my age" tables you see online. I could not find published figures behind those either, so this page does not print any.

🥗 Nutrition

For most teenagers who train, the problem is not eating too much. It is not eating enough.

The Finding That Turns This Page Around

Most muscle-building food advice online is written for adults. They are usually trying to gain weight slowly while staying lean.

The research on teenagers points the other way. A 2025 review found that teenage athletes often do not eat enough to cover what they burn. One study it mentions looked at young footballers. They ate around 1,900 calories a day. They were burning around 3,600.

Not eating enough for a long time messes with your hormones. The review lists what can follow: puberty starting later, periods stopping, weaker bones, ending up shorter than you would have been, eating problems, and more injuries.

⚠️ Worth reading twice People warn teenagers that training will stunt their growth. There is a real growth risk here. It is just attached to the wrong thing. It is not the weights. It is not eating enough while you train hard.

Source: Everett (2025), Nutrients — a review of nutrition for teenage athletes.

How Much a Training Teenager Needs

These are rough daily amounts from a group of children's doctors. They change with your size, how active you are, and where you are in your growth.

2,000–3,200

1,800–2,400

This site does not tell you to eat a set amount extra. You will often see advice to eat 250 to 500 calories a day above what you burn. That comes from adult bodybuilding, and I could not find anything supporting it for teenagers.

Since not eating enough is the common problem, "eat regular meals, and enough of them" is better advice at your age than doing sums. If you want a number for you specifically, ask a GP or a dietitian. The research says sending teenage athletes to a sports dietitian should be a normal part of looking after them.

Sources: Hecht et al. (2024), JPOSNA; Everett (2025), Nutrients.

Protein — Less Than You Are Told, Spread Out More

Two separate groups of researchers looked at this and landed close together. One says about 1.4 to 2.0 g of protein per kg of bodyweight per day. The other says about 1.5 g per kg.

🎯 The bit most people get wrong is not the total Both sources say it matters more that you spread protein across the day than that you hit an exact daily number. Roughly 20 to 40 g at each meal, every three to four hours, works well.

One study found teenagers eating 68% of their daily protein at dinner and only 14% at breakfast. That is the mistake to avoid.

And here is the finding that makes the supplement aisle look different. Teenage athletes usually already eat plenty of protein. Often two to three times what they need. Without any powder.

Good sources of protein

Chicken and other lean meat, fish including tinned tuna, eggs, Greek yoghurt, cottage cheese, milk, lentils, beans, chickpeas, tofu and nuts.

For exact amounts per portion, use an official food database rather than a fitness site or a supplement shop. This site does not print its own protein table, because it has no proper source for one.

Sources: Everett (2025), Nutrients; Hecht et al. (2024), JPOSNA. The 1.6–2.2 g/kg figure quoted on many fitness sites is the adult one.

Carbs — The Forgotten One

Muscle-building content talks almost entirely about protein. The teenage research puts carbohydrate at 6 to 10 g per kg of bodyweight per day for young athletes.

That is far more than most training content mentions. The reason is simple: most training content is aimed at adults trying to lose fat, not teenagers trying to grow.

Source: Everett (2025), Nutrients.

Habits That Are Actually Backed Up

  • Eat a meal or a snack before training, and again afterwards.
  • Eat breakfast every day. Teenagers who do have a better diet overall.
  • Spread your protein across the day instead of piling it into the evening meal.
  • Food first. The research says supplements should never replace proper meals.
  • Judge your progress by what you can do and how much energy you have, not by how you look or what the scales say. The review recommends this on purpose, because focusing on weight and appearance raises the risk of eating problems.

Sources: Hecht et al. (2024), JPOSNA; Everett (2025), Nutrients.

⚠️ Not Eating Enough (RED-S)

RED-S stands for Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport. It is the name for what happens when you keep taking in less energy than your training and your growing need.

In a body that is still developing, the effects are the ones listed further up this page: hormones thrown off, weaker bones, puberty delayed, and more injuries.

If you train hard and feel wiped out all the time, or keep getting injured, eating more is a more likely fix than training more. If food or eating is something you find hard or stressful, that is worth talking to your GP about. Lots of people need help with it. There is nothing strange about asking.

Source: Everett (2025), Nutrients.

💊 Supplements

Two different sets of doctors, coming at this from different directions, reach the same answer.

🥦 The starting position, and it is not a soft one A group of doctors who specialise in children's bones and joints says young athletes should not take supplements to improve performance, unless a doctor says there is a medical reason. A 2025 review of teenage nutrition reaches the same place from a different direction: food first, and supplements should never replace proper meals.

Two groups. Two routes. One answer.

Sources: Hecht et al. (2024), JPOSNA; Everett (2025), Nutrients.

The Full Picture

❌ Whey Protein

Not harmful. Usually just pointless. The research says protein shakes are not recommended for teenagers, because teenagers already eat more protein than they need. Often two to three times more.

Powder is a convenient way to get protein. It is not a better way.

Not needed — food first

⚠️ Creatine

Experts disagree about this one. You deserve to know that, rather than be handed a simple answer.

The children's doctors say under-18s should not take it. Their reason is that nobody has properly tested it in this age group, not that harm has been proven. A 2025 teenage nutrition review says it could be considered, but only under strict conditions. The bone and joint doctors point to studies where it improved performance with no bad effects. Fitness sites that sell things tend to recommend it flatly, without telling you experts disagree.

Not settled — a decision for you and a doctor

✅ Vitamin D

The one real exception, and it is nothing to do with muscle.

The NHS says people in the UK should think about taking vitamin D every day in autumn and winter. There is not enough sunlight in those months for your body to make its own. Check the NHS page for how much they currently recommend.

Worth considering Oct–March (NHS)

❌ BCAAs

Heavily advertised. No source in this project looks at BCAAs specifically, so this one is covered by the general rule above rather than by its own study.

What the sources do say: supplements are not recommended without a medical reason, and teenagers already eat more protein than they need. The building blocks sold in BCAA products are already in that protein.

Not recommended

❌ Pre-Workout & Energy Drinks

There is a number for this one. Doctors say teenagers should have no more than 100 mg of caffeine a day. That is about one mug of coffee. A single scoop of most pre-workout powders is over that on its own.

Energy drinks come out worse. The research says they have no health benefit at all and are not recommended for teenagers.

There is also a loophole worth understanding. There are legal limits on caffeine in fizzy drinks. But products sold as supplements do not have to follow those limits. The dose is whatever the company decided to put in.

Avoid

❌ Fat Burners

No source in this project has anything to say about fat burners. That silence is itself the answer.

They fall under the general rule: no supplements for under-18s to improve performance or change how they look, without a medical reason. They are also stimulant products sold to under-18s through the same loophole described above.

Avoid completely

Sources: Seifert et al. (2011), Pediatrics for the caffeine limit and the supplement loophole (note this source is from 2011, and the pre-workout market has changed since); Everett (2025), Nutrients; Hecht et al. (2024), JPOSNA; Stricker et al. (2020), Pediatrics.

What This Site Will Not Cover

Peptides get talked about alongside supplements. They are not a food question. They are a drug question, and none of the sources in this project covers them. Guessing at an answer would be worse than saying nothing.

The children's doctors' report treats performance-enhancing drugs as something to warn young people about, in order to put them off using them. It also tells doctors to watch out for their use in teenagers who are chasing muscle.

If this is something you are thinking about, that conversation belongs with a doctor. Not with a website written by a sixth-former.

Source: Stricker, Faigenbaum & McCambridge (2020), Pediatrics.

Before taking any supplement, speak to a parent or guardian and ask a doctor. The children's health sources used here say under-18s should only take supplements when there is a medical reason, and that you should check with a healthcare professional first. This page is educational and is not medical or dietary advice.

🧠 Mental Health

Training changes how you feel. It is worth being exact about which parts of that the evidence backs up.

What the Research on Young People Shows

In 2025, researchers pooled 27 trials covering just over 3,000 children and teenagers. What makes this study unusual is that it compared different types of exercise against each other, not just against doing nothing.

For low mood and depression, weight training came out on top. It beat running-type exercise, mind-and-body exercise like yoga, and mixed programmes. For self-esteem, weight training came first again.

For anxiety, they found no clear effect. The result was too uncertain to say training helped at all. You will see it claimed everywhere that exercise helps with anxiety. In young people, that has not been shown, so this site does not claim it.

How strong is this evidence? The researchers rate their own evidence as low to moderate quality. The effects are real in direction, but small. This is not a huge pile of research.

Source: Zhou et al. (2025), BMC Public Health — a review pooling 27 trials in children and adolescents.

The Effect That Came Out Biggest

Another review pulled apart the different things people mean by "confidence". It turns out they do not all move together.

The biggest change by far was in believing you can actually train. Researchers call this self-efficacy. How young people felt about what their body could do went up by a smaller amount. How they felt about themselves in general went up by less again.

That pattern makes sense. The gym gives you something most of your life does not: a place where effort turns into a result you can see, on a timescale you can actually notice, in a number you produced yourself. School gives you feedback in months, in a grade someone else decided. A training log gives it back in days.

That is why keeping a log is not an optional extra on this site. It is the part with the best evidence behind it.

Source: Collins et al. (2019), Sports Medicine – Open — seven studies, ages 10–16, described by its own authors as a small set of mixed quality.

The Result Worth Reading Twice

In that same review, three things did not change. One of them was how young people felt about their appearance.

So the evidence says training reliably improves how you feel about what your body can do. It does not show that it will make you happier with how your body looks.

A huge amount of fitness content sells exactly that second promise. It is the one thing the research did not find.

💡 Which is why this site is written the way it is Progress here is about what you can now do, not how you now look. That is not just a kinder way to put it. It is the better-evidenced one. The teenage nutrition research separately recommends focusing on performance and energy rather than weight and appearance, because the other way round raises the risk of eating problems.

Sources: Collins et al. (2019), Sports Medicine – Open; Everett (2025), Nutrients.

Discipline Beats Motivation

Motivation is a feeling, and feelings come and go. If you wait to feel motivated before training, you will train patchily.

Discipline is closer to a habit. It means showing up on the days you do not fancy it.

James Clear's book Atomic Habits makes the case that your results come from the routines you build, rather than from how ambitious your goals are. That is a useful way to think about turning up.

This one is an idea, not evidence. Atomic Habits is a popular book, not research. That is why it is listed separately from the scientific sources. It is here because it puts the idea well, not because anyone tested it.

When Training Stops Helping

Everything above measures the good side, in supervised programmes. None of it studies what happens when training turns into something driven by how you look, or becomes something you feel you cannot skip.

The research not covering that does not mean it does not happen.

Two sources on this site point at it from the side. The teenage nutrition review warns that young athletes are easily influenced by things around them, which can raise the risk of eating problems. The children's doctors' report tells doctors to watch for unusual eating, an intense focus on body image, and drug use in teenagers chasing muscle.

⚠️ If training is making you feel worse rather than better If missing a session makes you genuinely anxious, if your thoughts about how you look have turned harsh, or if any of this is affecting how you eat, talk to someone. A parent, a teacher you trust, or your GP.

The benefits described on this page are small, and come from evidence the researchers themselves call low to moderate quality. Weight training is not a treatment for mental illness, and nothing on this page should replace real help.

Sources: Everett (2025), Nutrients; Stricker et al. (2020), Pediatrics.

🛡️ Safety for Teenagers

The strongest evidence on this site, and one statistic that needed checking properly.

Is Weight Training Safe for Teenagers?

Yes — as long as it is supervised, technique first, and built up gradually. That condition applies to everything else on this page.

The clearest answer comes from the children's doctors — the American Academy of Pediatrics, a large group of doctors who specialise in treating children and teenagers. Part of their report is a table listing things people commonly believe, with the research next to each one. Most of the myths on this page come straight from it.

Source: Stricker, Faigenbaum & McCambridge (2020), Pediatrics — AAP clinical report, reaffirmed November 2024. It replaces the AAP's 2008 statement on the same subject.

Injury Rates: What the Numbers Really Say

You will see it said that weight training cuts sports injury risk by up to 66%. That figure gets passed around fitness websites, which are quoting research they rarely link to.

I traced it back to the original paper. What is in there is more complicated, and more interesting, so this page shows what the paper actually says instead.

The paper gives two different injury rates for weight training in young people. They are far apart, because they come from different kinds of study.

FigureInjuries per 100 hours of trainingWhere it comes from
Weight training (followed forwards)0.176, 0.053, 0.055Studies that followed people over time and recorded injuries as they happened
Weight training (looking back)0.0035A 1994 study that asked over 4,000 young lifters to report past injuries
Rugby, for comparison0.800The comparison table in that same 1994 study

Compare like with like, using the 1994 study's own numbers, and weight training comes out around two hundred times lower than rugby. Compare it against the more careful follow-forward numbers, and the gap is closer to five to fifteen times.

Either way the direction is the same. And it is worth noticing which activity adults usually worry about.

Why both numbers are here. Quoting only the smallest one would make the comparison look more dramatic than the evidence allows. Quoting only the biggest would undersell what the paper found. Showing which type of study produced which number is the honest version.

Source: Faigenbaum & Myer (2010), British Journal of Sports Medicine.

Growth Plates — The Facts

Growth plates are areas of soft cartilage near the ends of your long bones. They are where the bone grows longer. Once you stop growing, they harden.

The worry is that loading them while they are still soft causes damage and stunts your growth.

📚 What two separate sources say The children's doctors' report lists "weight training stunts your growth" as a myth. Well-designed programmes have not been shown to harm growth plates, height, or heart health in young people.

The injury review adds that no study following young people forwards, with proper coaching, has ever reported a growth plate injury. It also says there is no evidence weight training harms your height.

The injuries that do appear in the research have something in common. They involve training with nobody supervising, poor technique, and maximum-effort lifts.

So the worry behind the myth is not stupid. The answer it points to is coaching, not avoiding the gym.

What to avoid

  • Testing your maximum lift, especially with nobody qualified watching
  • Big jumps in weight
  • Adding weight before you have learned the movement
  • Training alone as a complete beginner

Sources: Stricker et al. (2020), Pediatrics; Faigenbaum & Myer (2010), British Journal of Sports Medicine.

How long before judging a programmeAt least 8 weeksStricker et al. (2020)

You should be able to do the whole set with good form. If your form falls apart, the weight is too heavy.

This site never tells you to lift a percentage of your maximum. The children's doctors say finding out your maximum should only happen with proper supervision and proper testing. And getting bigger does not need heavy weights anyway.

Sources: Stricker et al. (2020), Pediatrics; Zwolski, Quatman-Yates & Paterno (2017), Sports Health.

The One Thing That Decides Safety

It is not your age. It is not how heavy you lift. It is not which exercises you pick.

Every source on this site written about young people points to the same thing: proper supervision. It is what separates the safe results from the injury reports that started the fear in the first place.

None of the reassuring numbers above came from teenagers training alone and going for maximum lifts. They should not be quoted as if they did.

The children's doctors also say to check with a medical professional before you start. The NHS says young people should get medical advice about exercising if they have a health condition, and that they can use weights as long as a qualified adult teaches them and supervises them.

Sources: Stricker et al. (2020), Pediatrics; Faigenbaum & Myer (2010), BJSM; NHS, Physical activity guidelines for children and young people.

Aches and Pains

Aching muscles for a day or two after new training is normal. Sharp pain, joint pain, or anything that does not settle down is a different thing.

This site is not the right place to tell you which is which. Plenty of fitness sites have a table sorting normal aches from warning signs. That is a medical judgement, none of my sources supports one, and getting it wrong matters.

If something hurts in a way you are unsure about, stop that movement and speak to your GP or a sports physiotherapist. That is a shorter answer than the table was, and a more reliable one.

This page is general educational information and does not replace advice from a doctor. If you have any existing condition, past injury, or worry, speak to a doctor or a qualified sports medicine professional before you start a programme.

📋 Beginner's Programme

Three days a week, full body, built from the guidance on the Safety page.

Read this before the tables: nobody published this programme. No study has tested this exact routine, and none of my sources lists a specific set of exercises.

What the sources do give are the settings: how often to train, how many sets, how many reps, how many exercises, and how to start. Every one of those comes from the guidance on the Safety page. How I arranged the exercises inside those settings is my choice. Where something here is just habit rather than evidence, I have said so.

How to Progress

WeeksWhat to focus on
1–2Technique only. Light weight or none. Learn every movement.
3–4Add a small amount of weight where your form is solid.
5–8When a session feels manageable, add a rep, a set, or a little weight. All three count as progress.
9Easier week. Around half the usual weight, same exercises, focus on technique.
10+Start the cycle again from a higher base.

Eight weeks is the shortest sensible time to judge whether a programme is working. That is why the first review point is week nine and not earlier.

Week 9 is a habit, not a finding. None of my sources shows that easier weeks work. It is here because taking a week lighter is low-risk and lots of people do it. Skipping it is not a mistake.

Source for the 8-week minimum: Stricker et al. (2020), Pediatrics.

📚 Sources & References

Which sources I used, which I threw out, and how I decided.

How I Chose These Sources

I tested every source with five questions. Who wrote it, and what do they do for a living? Did anyone check it before it was published? How recent is it? Who pays for it, and is anything being sold? And does anything else say the same thing?

I then wrote up each source properly in my research notes. Every real claim on this site points back to one of them.

The lists below are kept separate, because different kinds of source are good and bad at different things. Research papers are closest to the evidence, but they are slow to read and a single study can be wrong. Reports from professional bodies weigh up all the evidence at once and have a duty of care, but they are cautious and can be slow to catch up. Commercial articles are readable and free, but the company behind them may want something from you.

An honest note about this site. In form, this website is a commercial-style source. It is free, readable, and written for the audience, even though nothing here is for sale. So how do you make it more trustworthy than that kind of site usually is? Cite the original research instead of copying someone else's summary. Say plainly when experts disagree. Follow the children's health bodies on anything medical. And tell readers when to go and ask a professional.

Limits of the Evidence on This Site

  • Most of the best training research leaves out under-18s. The biggest review I used was done entirely on adults. Where its numbers appear here, they are an educated guess for a teenager, and the page says so.
  • Several of the youth sources studied young athletes who were already coached and training, not beginners.
  • Two sources per topic was a limit I set myself. It is not a full review of everything published.
  • Some numbers here come from studies on boys, because that is how the original research reports them.
