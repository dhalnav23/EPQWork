Building Muscle as a Teenager

A simple guide based on research about muscle growth, safe training, food, recovery and mental health.

Why I made this website

I started going to the gym in the winter of 2025. When I searched for advice, I found lots of different answers on websites, social media and fitness videos. Some sources did not say whether their advice was suitable for teenagers.

This website shares what I found about building muscle while the body is still developing.

Strength training means making muscles work against a force from weights, bands or body weight. Research papers also call it resistance training.

Recovery means the body returning towards its normal condition after exercise. Self-esteem means how a person values themselves.

🔬 How Muscles Grow

What hypertrophy means and what makes a muscle get bigger.

⚡ The short version

  • Hypertrophy means an increase in muscle size.
  • Tension on the muscle is the main trigger for growth.
  • Teenagers may gain muscle, but the amount is uncertain.

What makes a muscle grow?

Muscle fibres are the long cells that make up a muscle. They can become thicker through training.

Mechanical tension is the force in a muscle when it works or stretches. Research identifies this as the main trigger for growth. Source 1

Becoming stronger does not always mean that a muscle has become much bigger. Research cannot predict how much muscle each teenager will gain. Source 2

What I learned Muscle growth takes time, and results differ. The research cannot promise a fixed gain for each teenager.
More about the evidence

What I thought before researching

I thought training worked by damaging muscle fibres, the long cells that make up a muscle. I believed that rest and food repaired the damage and made the muscle bigger.

What I found

Van Every and colleagues (2025) identify mechanical tension as the main trigger for muscle growth from strength training. Mechanical tension is the force in a muscle when it works or stretches. Source 1

This gave me a different explanation to consider: the review puts mechanical tension at the centre of muscle growth. It does not directly examine muscle damage, so I cannot use it to rule out my original idea about damage.

Van Every and colleagues explains how muscles grow, but it is not about teenagers. I needed a source that was closer to my own age. Source 1

Enomoto and Tottori (2025) examined 29 studies of muscle size in children and teenagers. Only nine tested a training programme. Some studies found muscle growth, but the results differed. Source 2

Differences in growth, training and ways of measuring muscle size make the results hard to compare. There was also less research on girls. The review cannot predict a fixed muscle gain for each teenager.

Sources: Source 1, Source 2.

🛡️ Is Resistance Training Safe for Teenagers?

What the research says about injuries, growth and growth plates.

⚡ The short version

  • Strength training can be safe with a suitable programme, correct technique and qualified supervision.
  • Good technique matters more than lifting a heavy weight.
  • Properly planned training with qualified supervision has not been shown to stunt growth or damage growth plates.

What safe training involves

Strength training makes muscles work against weights, bands or body weight. Qualified supervision means that someone with suitable training watches and helps correct mistakes.

Growth plates are areas near the ends of growing bones where bones increase in length.

Injuries can still happen. Learn the correct movement, use a weight you can control and increase it slowly. Source 4

What I learned Resistance training can be safe for teenagers when it is properly planned and supervised by qualified professionals. They should learn the correct technique, start with a weight they can control and increase it slowly.
More about the evidence

What I found

The UK Chief Medical Officers recommend varied activities for children aged 5 to 18. These should develop movement skills, muscle fitness and bone strength. This guidance does not give a detailed gym programme. Source 3

Lloyd and colleagues (2014) says that resistance training can be safe when it is planned properly and supervised by qualified professionals. Properly planned training with qualified supervision has not been shown to stunt growth or damage growth plates. Source 4

Injuries can still happen. The risk may be higher when someone uses poor technique, lifts a weight they cannot control or trains without supervision.

Sources: Source 3, Source 4.

📈 How Should Teenagers Train?

A simple starting point for safe resistance training.

⚡ The short version

  • Learn each movement with a qualified coach before adding more weight.
  • Use a weight you can control with good technique.
  • General guidance suggests two or three sessions a week, with rest days between.
  • Increase the difficulty slowly.

Know the terms

Strength training makes muscles work against a force. Bodyweight exercises use your own weight. Free weights, such as dumbbells, can move freely.

A repetition is one complete exercise movement. A set is a group of repetitions before a rest.

Build up slowly

Progressive overload means increasing the demands of training over time. This can mean adding a repetition or a small amount of weight when ready.

Better control is useful progress even when the weight stays the same. Age alone does not show that you are ready for heavier weights. Source 5

Two or three sessions a week is general guidance, not a fixed plan for everyone. Other sport and exercise also matter when planning rest. Source 4

Keep it simple Technique comes first. If you cannot control the weight, it is too heavy for that exercise.
More about the evidence

What the reviews say

Lloyd and colleagues (2014) recommend learning each movement with little or no weight before adding resistance. Source 4

McQuilliam and colleagues (2020) reviewed young athletes at different stages of growth and with different training experience. Heavier training depends on physical development, exercise skill and qualified coaching.

Physical development means how far the body has developed towards adulthood. Advice for athletes will not always suit beginners. Source 5

Sources: Source 4, Source 5.

🥗 Nutrition for Muscle Development

Eating enough food for growth, training and recovery.

⚡ The short version

  • Teenagers need enough food for normal growth as well as training.
  • A varied, balanced diet should come before supplements.
  • Carbohydrate gives the body energy for exercise.
  • Protein helps to build and repair muscle.

Eat enough and eat a range of foods

Recovery means the body returning towards its normal condition after exercise. Eating too little can affect recovery and increase injury risk. Source 6

A balanced diet can usually provide enough protein when you also eat enough food. Source 7

Food before supplements

Supplements are products taken alongside normal food, such as protein powders. They can help in some cases, but cannot make up for poor food choices.

Before using one, get advice from a registered health or nutrition professional, such as a registered dietitian. Source 7

What about creatine?

Creatine is a substance stored in muscles that helps supply energy during short periods of hard exercise.

Research on teenagers is limited. It is not a supplement for everyone. Get professional advice before using it. Source 6 Source 7

What I learned Eat enough, eat a range of foods and do not treat protein powder as a replacement for meals.
More about the evidence

Who the research covers

Everett (2025) reviews teenage athletes who train often. Low energy availability means too little energy remains for normal body functions after exercise. It can affect recovery and increase injury risk.

The review is not a personal eating plan for every teenager. Source 6

Everett describes limited research on creatine in teenagers and conditions for use by teenage athletes. This does not justify saying all experts agree for or against its use. Source 6

A doctor can help with a health concern. This website gives general information, not a personal eating or supplement plan.

Sources: Source 6, Source 7.

😴 Rest and Pre-Workout Products

Why recovery matters and why high-caffeine drinks need care.

⚡ The short version

  • Rest days give the body time to recover.
  • Sport and other exercise also count towards the total amount of activity.
  • Energy-drink guidance does not cover every pre-workout product.
  • Energy drinks do not replace rest.

Leave time for recovery

Recovery means the body returning towards its normal condition after exercise. General guidance suggests two or three strength sessions a week, with rest days between.

Include other sport and exercise when planning rest. Source 4

Check pre-workout products

Pre-workout products are sold for use before exercise. Some contain caffeine, a stimulant that can make you feel more alert.

High-caffeine energy drinks carry a warning that they are not recommended for children. Products differ, so this guidance does not cover every pre-workout product. Source 8

Get advice from a registered health or nutrition professional before using a supplement. Source 7

What I learned Rest is part of training. Teenagers should avoid high-caffeine energy drinks and get advice before using a pre-workout supplement.
More about the evidence

What the government decision covers

The government decision records a planned ban on sales of high-caffeine energy drinks to children under 16 in England. It does not show that the ban has started.

The planned rules include some powders mixed into drinks, but do not cover every pre-workout product. Public responses alone do not prove health effects. Source 8

Sources: Source 4, Source 7, Source 8.

🧠 Mental Health Benefits

What resistance training may help, and what the research does not prove.

⚡ The short version

  • Exercise may help low mood and self-esteem in young people.
  • Resistance training may improve confidence in the ability to train.
  • The evidence does not clearly show that it reduces anxiety.
  • Exercise is not a replacement for professional mental health support.

Confidence and how you feel

Strength training makes muscles work against weights, bands or body weight. Self-esteem means how you value yourself.

Self-efficacy means confidence in doing a particular task, such as strength training. Body image means how you think and feel about your body.

Research suggests possible benefits for confidence and self-esteem. Results for anxiety and feelings about appearance are less clear. This does not prove that nobody can benefit. Source 9 Source 10

What I learned Resistance training may improve confidence and self-esteem. Possible benefits are not a promise that everyone will feel better. Anyone struggling with their mental health should speak to a GP, counsellor or another trusted professional.
More about the evidence

What the studies found

Zhou and colleagues (2025) combined results from 27 trials involving 3,059 children and teenagers. A trial tests an activity or treatment and measures the results. The review found possible benefits for depression symptoms and self-esteem. Source 9

The combined results gave no clear evidence of an overall anxiety benefit. This does not prove that exercise never helps anxiety. The authors rated the evidence as low or moderate, so it cannot confidently identify the best exercise type for everyone.

Collins and colleagues (2019) reviewed seven studies of children aged 10 to 16 and combined results from four. These results showed improvements in confidence about training and feelings of self-worth. Source 10

There was no statistically significant improvement in feelings about body attractiveness. This means there was no clear evidence of that improvement, not proof that nobody can feel better about their appearance. Only two studies received a strong quality rating.

Sources: Source 9, Source 10.

Bibliography

The sources used, what I learned and the limits of the evidence.

How to read the sources

Sources 1 to 10 provide the health information. The final entry explains a source I checked but did not use as evidence.

A review brings together earlier studies. Its conclusions depend on those studies and how the authors compare them. A journal publication does not guarantee that every claim is correct.

In article titles, resistance training means strength training, and adolescents means teenagers. A scoping review maps the research available. A systematic review uses a planned method to find and compare studies.

A meta-analysis uses statistics to combine study results. A network meta-analysis compares several options using results from different studies. A narrative review explains research without necessarily using a systematic search.

Peer review means that other specialists checked an article before publication. The term et al. means “and colleagues”. Neither a source's age nor an author's job alone decides its reliability.

1. Van Every and colleagues (2025)

Van Every, D.W., Lees, M.J., Wilson, B., Nippard, J. and Phillips, S.M. (2025, first published online) 'Load-induced human skeletal muscle hypertrophy: Mechanisms, myths, and misconceptions', Journal of Sport and Health Science, 15, 101104. Published in volume 15 (2026). Read article.

This article brings together research on how muscles grow. I use it to explain mechanical tension, which is the force in a muscle when it works or stretches. The authors explain that this tension is the main trigger for muscle growth from strength training. The article gives links to research, so I can check the evidence behind its claims. Two authors say they work in the fitness industry, which could affect their views. I think it is useful for explaining muscle growth, but it cannot tell each teenager how much muscle they will gain.

2. Enomoto and Tottori (2025)

Enomoto, S. and Tottori, N. (2025) 'Muscle morphological adaptations to resistance training and sports participation in children and adolescents: a scoping review', Frontiers in Sports and Active Living, 7, 1646835. Read article.

This review looks at the research available on muscle size in children and teenagers. It includes 29 studies, but only nine tested a training programme. Some studies found that muscles grew, but the results were not the same in every study. The authors explain how they searched three research databases, so readers can see how they found the studies. Differences in growth, training and ways of measuring muscle size make the results hard to compare. I use it to show that teenagers can build muscle, but the amount of growth is uncertain.

3. UK Chief Medical Officers (2026)

Department of Health and Social Care (2026) UK Chief Medical Officers' physical activity guidelines. Updated 10 July 2026. Available at: Read source

This source gives health advice from the four UK Chief Medical Officers. For children aged 5 to 18, it recommends a mix of activities across the week. These activities should improve movement skills, muscle fitness and bone strength. I use it to explain why activities that strengthen muscles and bones are part of a healthy lifestyle. I think it is useful because it gives the research behind the advice. But it does not say which weights, sets or repetitions a teenager should use in the gym.

4. Lloyd and colleagues (2014)

Lloyd, R.S. et al. (2014) 'Position statement on youth resistance training: the 2014 International Consensus', British Journal of Sports Medicine, 48(7), pp. 498-505. Read article.

This source brings together research and advice from experts in different countries on strength training for children and teenagers. It explains that training can be safe with a qualified person supervising, correct technique and a suitable programme. I use it to explain these safety points. I think it is useful because the authors give research references and clear training advice. But it dates from 2014, and agreement between experts does not prove that every exercise is safe in every setting.

5. McQuilliam and colleagues (2020)

McQuilliam, S.J., Clark, D.R., Erskine, R.M. and Brownlee, T.E. (2020) 'Free-weight resistance training in youth athletes: a narrative review', Sports Medicine, 50, pp. 1567-1580. Read article.

This article brings together research on training with free weights in young athletes. It looks at strength and power at different stages of growth. The authors recommend making training harder gradually, with correct technique and a qualified coach. Their advice on heavier weights depends on how far an athlete has developed physically and how well they can do the exercises. I use it to explain why age alone does not show that a teenager is ready for heavier weights. I think the research references make it useful, but advice for athletes will not always suit a beginner.

6. Everett (2025)

Everett, S. (2025) 'Optimizing performance nutrition for adolescent athletes: A review of dietary needs, risks, and practical strategies', Nutrients, 17(17), 2792. Read article.

This article brings together research on food and nutrition for teenage athletes. It explains that teenagers must eat enough to give them energy for growth, training and recovery. It also explains the health risks of eating too little. I use it to explain why enough food is important when a teenager trains. The article gives research references, but it does not give a clear search method or explain how it checked each study's quality. Its general advice agrees with source 7, but its suggested amounts of food and nutrients will not suit every teenager.

7. British Dietetic Association (2026)

British Dietetic Association (2026) 'Sport and exercise'. May. Available at: Read source

This is a public information sheet from the British Dietetic Association. It explains that a varied, balanced diet should give athletes the energy and nutrients they need. Supplements cannot make up for poor food choices, but they can help in some cases. I use it to check the general food advice in source 6. I think it is useful because it names the nutrition experts who wrote it and gives research references. But it gives general advice, so it cannot tell an individual teenager exactly what to eat.

8. Department of Health and Social Care (2026)

Department of Health and Social Care (2026) 'Banning the sale of high-caffeine energy drinks to children: consultation outcome'. Updated 16 July 2026. Available at: Read source

This source is the government's own record of its decision about energy drinks with high levels of caffeine. It explains that these drinks carry a warning that they are not recommended for children. I use it for this warning and the planned ban on sales to children under 16 in England. It records a plan to introduce a law, so it does not show that the ban has already started. It is useful for checking that decision, but public responses alone do not prove that these drinks cause health problems. The planned ban includes some powders mixed into drinks, but it does not cover every product sold for use before exercise.

9. Zhou and colleagues (2025)

Zhou, S., Xu, Y., Yang, Y., Song, H., Wang, X. and Yu, Y. (2025) 'Effect of exercise interventions on depression, anxiety, and self-esteem in children and adolescents: a systematic review and network meta-analysis', BMC Public Health, 25, 3706. Read article.

This review brings together results from several studies to compare different types of exercise. It includes 27 trials with 3,059 children and teenagers. The results show possible benefits for depression symptoms and self-esteem, but no clear evidence of an overall anxiety benefit. The authors explain how they found the studies and checked their quality. They rate the evidence as low or moderate because the studies differ and some methods could give misleading results. I use it to explain possible mental health benefits, but it does not show that every teenager will benefit.

10. Collins and colleagues (2019)

Collins, H., Booth, J.N., Duncan, A., Fawkner, S. and Niven, A. (2019) 'The effect of resistance training interventions on "the self" in youth: a systematic review and meta-analysis', Sports Medicine - Open, 5, 29. Read article.

This review looks at strength training and how children and teenagers feel about themselves. It includes seven studies of children aged 10 to 16, and combines the results from four of these studies. The combined results showed improvements in confidence about strength training and feelings of self-worth. But they did not give clear evidence that participants felt better about how their bodies looked. The authors checked the quality of the studies, but only two received a strong rating. I use it to explain possible benefits for confidence and self-esteem, without promising that training will improve body image.

StrengthLog: checked but not used as evidence

Abelsson, A. (2025) 'Strength Training for Teens: The Ultimate Guide', StrengthLog. Last reviewed 9 January 2026. Available at: Read source

This fitness website article gives training advice and links to research. It is easy to read, but the company that publishes it also sells a training app. The article recommends creatine for everyone without clearly explaining the limits of the research on teenagers. Source 6 gives a more careful view, with limited research and conditions for use by teenage athletes. I therefore use research papers and professional advice as evidence for my website. I have kept this article in the list to show that I checked it during the project.

Limits of my research Some studies concern adults or young athletes rather than teenage beginners. Puberty, the stage when the body develops towards adulthood, can affect results. The research cannot promise a fixed muscle gain or the same mental health benefit for everyone.
