Building Muscle as a Teenager

A simple guide based on research about muscle growth, safe training, food, recovery and mental health.

Why I made this website

I started going to the gym in the winter of 2025. When I searched for advice, I found lots of different answers on websites, social media and fitness videos. Some sources did not say whether their advice was suitable for teenagers.

I wanted to find out what the evidence says about building muscle safely while the body is still developing. This website gives the main points from my research.

Conclusion

My research shows that teenagers can build muscle safely. The safest approach is to learn the correct technique, use a weight that can be controlled, increase the difficulty slowly and leave time for recovery.

Muscle growth is mainly caused by tension on the muscle, not simply by damaging it. Teenagers also need enough food for growth and training. Supplements and high-caffeine drinks are not a replacement for food or rest.

Resistance training may help confidence, self-esteem and low mood, but it does not clearly reduce anxiety and it is not a replacement for professional mental health support.

Important This is a school project and gives general information. It does not replace advice from a doctor, physiotherapist, registered dietitian or qualified coach.

🔬 How Muscles Grow

What hypertrophy means and what makes a muscle get bigger.

⚡ The short version

  • Hypertrophy means an increase in muscle size.
  • Tension on the muscle is the main trigger for growth.
  • Muscle damage can happen, but it is not the main reason a muscle grows.
  • Teenagers can build muscle, but the amount will be different for each person.

What I thought before researching

I thought weight training worked by damaging muscle fibres. I believed that rest and protein repaired the damage and made the muscle bigger and stronger. I had also heard words such as hypertrophy, progressive overload and time under tension, but I was not sure what they meant.

What I found

Hypertrophy means that a muscle gets bigger. This happens when its fibres become thicker over time.

Van Every et al. (2025) says that tension on a muscle is the main trigger for growth. Tension is the force placed on a muscle when it works against resistance. Repeating this type of training can make the muscle fibres thicker.

This changed my original idea. Muscle damage can happen after training, especially after a new exercise, but tension seems to be more important.

Van Every et al. explains how muscles grow, but it is not about teenagers. I needed a source that was closer to my own age.

Enomoto and Tottori (2025) looked at research on children and teenagers. Some studies found an increase in muscle size and others found little change. This means teenagers can build muscle, but it is difficult to say how much each person will gain.

Only a few studies tested an actual training programme. Some used small groups, many did not say what stage of puberty the teenagers were at, and there was less research on girls. These problems make the results less certain.

What I learned My first explanation was too simple. Tension is the main trigger for muscle growth. Teenagers can build muscle, but there is not enough research to predict the same result for everyone.

Sources: 1. Van Every et al. (2025) and 2. Enomoto and Tottori (2025).

🛡️ Is Resistance Training Safe for Teenagers?

What the research says about injuries, growth and growth plates.

⚡ The short version

  • Resistance training can be safe for teenagers.
  • It should be planned properly and supervised.
  • Good technique matters more than lifting a heavy weight.
  • Well-planned training has not been shown to stunt growth or damage growth plates.

Why I looked into this

After learning how muscles grow, I wanted to know if lifting weights is safe for teenagers. I was worried that it might cause injuries or affect growth. I looked for UK information relevant to children and teenagers.

I had heard that lifting weights could damage growth plates, but I did not know what growth plates were. I found that they are areas near the ends of bones where growth happens. Damage to them could affect how a bone grows, so I wanted to find out if weight training could cause this.

What I found

The UK Chief Medical Officers say that young people should do activities that make their muscles and bones stronger.

Lloyd et al. (2014) says that resistance training can be safe when it is planned properly and supervised. Well-planned training has not been shown to stunt growth or damage growth plates.

Injuries can still happen. The risk may be higher when someone uses poor technique, lifts a weight they cannot control or trains without supervision.

What I learned Resistance training can be safe for teenagers. They should learn the correct technique, start with a weight they can control and increase it slowly.

Sources: 3. UK Chief Medical Officers’ physical activity guidelines and 4. Lloyd et al. (2014).

📈 How Should Teenagers Train?

A simple starting point for safe resistance training.

⚡ The short version

  • Learn each movement before adding more weight.
  • Use a weight you can control with good technique.
  • Train two or three times a week, with rest days in between.
  • Increase the difficulty slowly.

Starting safely

I wanted to know how often teenagers should train, how heavy they should lift and how progressive overload works.

Lloyd et al. (2014) says that beginners should learn the correct movement first. They can start with little or no weight and add resistance when they can keep good form.

The same source recommends two or three sessions each week, with rest days between them.

Increasing the difficulty

Progressive overload means making training more difficult over time. This does not only mean adding weight. It can also mean doing another repetition, adding a set or doing the exercise with better control.

McQuilliam et al. (2020) discusses heavier training, but it focuses on young athletes who are already trained and properly coached. A beginner should not copy the loads used by an experienced athlete.

Keep it simple Technique comes first. If you cannot control the weight, it is too heavy for that exercise.

Sources: 4. Lloyd et al. (2014) and 5. McQuilliam et al. (2020).

🥗 Nutrition for Muscle Development

Eating enough food for growth, training and recovery.

⚡ The short version

  • Teenagers need enough food for normal growth as well as training.
  • A varied, balanced diet should come before supplements.
  • Carbohydrate gives the body energy for exercise.
  • Protein helps to build and repair muscle.

What matters most

Nutrition was one of the most confusing subjects I found online. Much of the advice focused on calorie counting, strict diets and supplements.

Everett (2025) says that teenagers need enough food for growth, training and recovery. Not eating enough can affect health, recovery and performance.

The British Dietetic Association says to eat a varied and balanced diet. Carbohydrate is an important source of energy for exercise, while protein helps muscles build and repair. A normal balanced diet can usually provide enough protein when the person is also eating enough food.

Food before supplements

Supplements cannot make up for a poor diet. The British Dietetic Association says that anyone considering a supplement should speak to a registered practitioner to check that it is suitable and safe.

A doctor may also be needed if there is a health concern. This is general safety advice, not a claim made by the BDA sport and exercise page.

What I learned Eat enough, eat a range of foods and do not treat protein powder as a replacement for meals.

Sources: 6. Everett (2025) and 7. British Dietetic Association (2026).

😴 Rest and Pre-Workout Products

Why recovery matters and why high-caffeine drinks need care.

⚡ The short version

  • Rest days give the body time to recover.
  • Sport and other exercise also count towards the total amount of activity.
  • Some pre-workout products contain a lot of caffeine.
  • A stimulant should not be used to cover up tiredness.

Rest between sessions

Earlier topics showed that resistance training can be safe for teenagers. I still had two questions. How much rest do teenagers need, and are pre-workout drinks safe?

Lloyd et al. (2014) recommends training two or three times a week, with rest days in between. Other exercise and sport should also be considered. The body needs time to recover.

High-caffeine drinks

Some pre-workout products contain large amounts of caffeine. The UK government says that high-caffeine energy drinks are linked with problems affecting children’s health, wellbeing, sleep and education.

The government plans to ban their sale to children under 16 in England from April 2027. The change is subject to Parliament, so it is not yet law.

The government information is about energy drinks, not every pre-workout product. Different products contain different ingredients. The British Dietetic Association says that people considering sports supplements should speak to a registered professional.

What I learned Rest is part of training. Teenagers should avoid high-caffeine energy drinks and get advice before using a pre-workout supplement.

Sources: 4. Lloyd et al. (2014), 7. British Dietetic Association (2026) and 8. Department of Health and Social Care (2026).

🧠 Mental Health Benefits

What resistance training may help, and what the research does not prove.

⚡ The short version

  • Exercise may help low mood and self-esteem in young people.
  • Resistance training may improve confidence in the ability to train.
  • The evidence does not clearly show that it reduces anxiety.
  • Exercise is not a replacement for professional mental health support.

What I wanted to find out

Before researching this topic, I thought resistance training could improve mood, reduce stress and build confidence. I wanted to find out if there was evidence for this.

What the studies found

Zhou et al. (2025) found that exercise may help with symptoms of depression and improve self-esteem in young people. Resistance training appeared to be particularly helpful for self-esteem. There was no clear improvement in anxiety.

Collins et al. (2019) looked only at resistance training. Young people became more confident in their ability to train. There were also small improvements in how they felt about themselves. However, it did not clearly improve how they felt about their appearance.

What I learned Resistance training may improve confidence and self-esteem. It may also help with low mood, but it will not have the same effect on everyone. Anyone struggling with their mental health should speak to a GP, counsellor or another trusted professional.

Sources: 9. Zhou et al. (2025) and 10. Collins et al. (2019).

📚 Bibliography

The sources used on this website, with the main point and a short reliability check.

1. Van Every et al. (2025)

Van Every, D.W., Lees, M.J., Wilson, B., Nippard, J. and Phillips, S.M. (2025) ‘Load-induced human skeletal muscle hypertrophy: Mechanisms, myths, and misconceptions’, Journal of Sport and Health Science, 15, 101104. doi:10.1016/j.jshs.2025.101104.

Main takeaway: Tension on a muscle is the main trigger for growth. This changed my original idea that muscle damage was the main cause.

Reliability: I think this is reliable because it was published in a scientific journal and uses evidence from many earlier studies. It is not about teenagers, so I only used it to explain the general process.

2. Enomoto and Tottori (2025)

Enomoto, S. and Tottori, N. (2025) ‘Muscle morphological adaptations to resistance training and sports participation in children and adolescents: a scoping review’, Frontiers in Sports and Active Living, 7, 1646835. doi:10.3389/fspor.2025.1646835.

Main takeaway: Teenagers can build muscle, but the amount will be different for each person.

Reliability: I think this is a useful and reliable starting point because it is a recent review about people under 18. Only a few studies tested a training programme, some groups were small and there was less research on girls. This means it cannot predict how much every teenager will gain.

3. Department of Health and Social Care (2026)

Department of Health and Social Care (2026) UK Chief Medical Officers’ physical activity guidelines. Updated 10 July 2026. Available on GOV.UK.

Main takeaway: Children and teenagers should do activities that develop movement skills and make their muscles and bones stronger.

Reliability: I think this is reliable because it is current guidance from the UK Chief Medical Officers. It is about general physical activity, so it does not give detailed instructions for lifting weights.

4. Lloyd et al. (2014)

Lloyd, R.S. et al. (2014) ‘Position statement on youth resistance training: the 2014 International Consensus’, British Journal of Sports Medicine, 48(7), pp. 498–505. doi:10.1136/bjsports-2013-092952.

Main takeaway: Resistance training can be safe for young people when it is suitable for their ability, properly taught and supervised.

Reliability: I think this is reliable because it was written by experts in youth training and sports medicine and was published in a medical journal. It is older, so I used it with current UK guidance.

5. McQuilliam et al. (2020)

McQuilliam, S.J., Clark, D.R., Erskine, R.M. and Brownlee, T.E. (2020) ‘Free-Weight Resistance Training in Youth Athletes: A Narrative Review’, Sports Medicine, 50(9), pp. 1567–1580. doi:10.1007/s40279-020-01307-7.

Main takeaway: Heavier training is more suitable for teenagers who are already trained and properly coached.

Reliability: I think this is reliable because it was written by UK university researchers and published in a scientific journal. It focuses on young athletes, so all of its advice may not suit a beginner.

6. Everett (2025)

Everett, S. (2025) ‘Optimizing performance nutrition for adolescent athletes: A review of dietary needs, risks, and practical strategies’, Nutrients, 17(17), 2792. doi:10.3390/nu17172792.

Main takeaway: Teenagers need enough food for growth, training and recovery.

Reliability: I think this is reliable because it is a recent review about adolescent athletes and was published in a scientific journal. It is an American review written by one author, so I compared its general advice with the British Dietetic Association.

7. British Dietetic Association (2026)

British Dietetic Association (2026) Sport and exercise. Published May 2026. Available from the BDA.

Main takeaway: A varied diet should provide energy for exercise and support recovery. Food comes before supplements.

Reliability: I think this is reliable because it is current UK guidance produced by registered nutrition professionals. It gives general sports nutrition advice and is not only about teenage muscle building.

8. Department of Health and Social Care (2026)

Department of Health and Social Care (2026) Banning the sale of high-caffeine energy drinks to children: consultation outcome. Published 16 July 2026. Available on GOV.UK.

Main takeaway: The government plans to ban the sale of high-caffeine energy drinks to children under 16 in England from April 2027.

Reliability: This is reliable for explaining the government’s decision and planned date. It is about energy drinks, not every type of pre-workout, and the change is still subject to Parliament.

9. Zhou et al. (2025)

Zhou, S., Xu, Y., Yang, Y., Song, H., Wang, X. and Yu, Y. (2025) ‘Effect of exercise interventions on depression, anxiety, and self-esteem in children and adolescents: a systematic review and network meta-analysis’, BMC Public Health, 25, 3706. doi:10.1186/s12889-025-25044-6.

Main takeaway: Exercise may improve low mood and self-esteem in young people, but it did not clearly improve anxiety.

Reliability: I think this is fairly reliable because it is a recent review that combined several studies. The authors rated the evidence as low to moderate, so the results are not certain.

10. Collins et al. (2019)

Collins, H., Booth, J.N., Duncan, A., Fawkner, S. and Niven, A. (2019) ‘The Effect of Resistance Training Interventions on “The Self” in Youth: a Systematic Review and Meta-analysis’, Sports Medicine – Open, 5, 29. doi:10.1186/s40798-019-0205-0.

Main takeaway: Resistance training may improve confidence and self-esteem in young people.

Reliability: I think this is reliable because it was written by UK university researchers and published in a scientific journal. It only found seven suitable studies, so the result is based on a small amount of research.

A website I checked but did not use as evidence

I also found a StrengthLog article written for teenagers. It was easy to understand and linked to scientific research. However, it was published by a company that sells a training app. It also recommended creatine without clearly explaining that guidance for teenagers is not agreed. I decided to use the original research and professional guidance instead.

Limits of my research Some studies were about adults or trained young athletes rather than teenage beginners. There were also fewer studies on teenagers than adults. Age, puberty, sex and training experience may affect the results.
